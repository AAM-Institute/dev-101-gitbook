# 8.0 Networking Basics

Your pages work. So let's look at the gap between someone typing your address and seeing your site, because almost everything that makes a site feel slow or jumpy happens in there.

You can watch all of it in the browser you already have open. Nothing to install.

### 8.1 The trip your page takes

<mark style="color:orange;">**Watch**</mark> [this five minute explainer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7_LPdttKXPc), then read [the Odin Project's version](https://www.theodinproject.com/lessons/foundations-how-does-the-web-work) and [MDN's](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn_web_development/Howto/Web_mechanics/How_does_the_Internet_work). Three passes at the same idea is on purpose.

The whole trip, in four steps:

1. **DNS.** The browser turns `yoursite.com` into a number like `104.198.14.52`. The network routes to the number. You remember the name. DNS is the phone book in between.
2. **Connection.** The browser opens a connection to that number. On `https` there's a quick handshake first to set up encryption.
3. **Request and response.** The browser asks for one file. The server answers with a status code and, if nothing went wrong, the file. `200` means here you go. `404` means no such file. `500` means the server broke.
4. **Render.** The browser reads your HTML top to bottom, and every `<link>`, `<script>`, `<img>` and font it finds starts *another* trip through steps 1 to 3.

Step 4 is the one people miss. A page is never one request. That business card page you built in 2.5 is probably ten.

Worth knowing about versions, since you'll hear them argued about: on HTTP/1.1 a browser could only hold a few connections open to one server, so requests waited in line, and jamming everything into one big file was the standard fix. HTTP/2 sends many requests down a single connection at once, so a pile of small files costs much less than it used to. [MDN has the full history](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTTP/Guides/Evolution_of_HTTP). Bookmark [the HTTP overview](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTTP/Guides/Overview) and [the status code list](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTTP/Reference/Status) too, you'll be back there for years.

### 8.2 The DNS records you'll actually touch

The first time you point a real domain at something you deployed, you're editing DNS records. There are dozens of types. You need these:

| Record    | What it does                                                                     |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **A**     | Points a name at an IPv4 address (`104.198.14.52`)                               |
| **AAAA**  | Same, for an IPv6 address                                                        |
| **CNAME** | Points a name at *another name*, which is how Netlify and Vercel usually want it |
| **MX**    | Where email for the domain goes                                                  |
| **TXT**   | Free-form text, mostly used to prove to a service that the domain is yours       |
| **NS**    | Which nameservers are in charge of the domain in the first place                 |

[Here's a plain-English rundown of every type](https://www.nslookup.io/learning/dns-record-types/), plus [MDN's glossary entry](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Glossary/DNS) for the short version.

**Try it.** Run `dig github.com` in your terminal on Mac or Linux, or `nslookup github.com` on Windows. The A record you just read about is right there. Now run it on your own deployed site.

### 8.3 Read your own page in the Network tab

This is the part I want you to actually do. It turns everything above from trivia into something you can see.

Open your business card page. Open developer tools (`Cmd + Option + I` on Mac, `F12` on Windows), click **Network**, and reload. Every row is one trip.

What to look at:

* **The row count and total size** at the bottom. That's what your page really costs.
* **Status.** Anything that isn't a `2xx` or `3xx` is a bug you can't catch by looking at the page.
* **Type and Size.** One image usually outweighs all your code put together.
* **The waterfall** on the right. A bar that starts late was discovered late, because the browser only learned it needed that file after reading something else.
* **The Throttling dropdown.** Set it to Slow 4G and reload. Now you're on your visitor's phone.

Reload a second time and watch rows flip to "disk cache" while the size drops. That's the browser skipping the trip entirely.

Panel docs: [Chrome](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/devtools/network/reference/), [Firefox](https://firefox-source-docs.mozilla.org/devtools-user/network_monitor/), and [Firefox's throttling guide](https://firefox-source-docs.mozilla.org/devtools-user/network_monitor/throttling/index.html). The tab looks nearly identical in both.

### 8.4 Why pages flash, jump, and feel slow

Two moments decide whether your site feels fast, and both have names.

[**First Contentful Paint**](https://web.dev/articles/fcp) is when anything at all shows up. [**Largest Contentful Paint**](https://web.dev/articles/lcp) is when the big thing shows up, usually a hero image or a headline. LCP is the one people feel, and [2.5 seconds is the line](https://web.dev/articles/optimize-lcp).

Four things get in the way. You've probably shipped all four already.

**CSS blocks rendering.** The browser refuses to paint until your stylesheet lands, deliberately, so nobody sees a flash of unstyled HTML. A fat stylesheet is a long blank screen.

**Scripts block parsing.** A plain `<script>` in the `<head>` stops the browser reading your HTML until that file downloads and runs. Add [`defer`](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Elements/script) and it waits politely until the page is parsed. Do that unless you have a specific reason not to.

**Web fonts arrive late.** The browser can't ask for a font until it knows some text needs it, which is after the CSS. So your text paints in a fallback, then repaints in your font, and the layout shifts because the two aren't the same size. [`font-display: swap`](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Reference/At-rules/@font-face/font-display) plus preloading the file takes most of that away. [These are the font rules worth following.](https://web.dev/articles/font-best-practices)

**Separate icon files arrive late too.** Every SVG in its own file is its own trip, and the space it will fill sits empty until it gets there. Inline the small ones straight into your HTML or CSS. And always set `width` and `height` on images so the browser reserves the space before the file exists.

All that jumping around has a score: [Cumulative Layout Shift](https://web.dev/articles/cls). If a page has ever slid the button out from under your thumb as you tapped it, you've met it.

Underneath everything in this section is one habit. **Load what the person sees first and let the rest wait.** [MDN's critical rendering path](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Performance/Guides/Critical_rendering_path) is the long version of that sentence.

### 8.5 Project: measure, change one thing, measure again

* Open the business card page from 2.5. ([Here's mine](https://aam-101-html.netlify.app/), and [the repo](https://github.com/AAM-Institute/project-002).)
* Network tab, throttling on Slow 4G, hard reload (`Cmd + Shift + R`, or `Ctrl + Shift + R`). Write down the request count, the kilobytes transferred, and the finish time.
* Change **one** thing. Add `width` and `height` to your image. Or `defer` a script. Or inline that SVG. Or set `font-display: swap`.
* Measure again and write down what moved.

One change, two sets of numbers. That's the whole exercise. The habit of measuring before and after will outlast every specific trick in this chapter.

**Bonus:** run Chrome's Lighthouse tab on the same page and see what it says to fix next.

### 8.6 If you want to go deeper

You don't need any of this for the rest of the course. It's here because people ask.

* [TCP](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Glossary/TCP) and [UDP](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Glossary/UDP) are two ways of moving data. TCP confirms everything arrived and in order, which is why the web runs on it. UDP doesn't bother, which is why calls and games prefer it. A socket is the open connection itself, the thing your code reads from and writes to.
* [WebSockets](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/WebSockets_API) hold a connection open in both directions. That's how a chat app or a live dashboard pushes you an update without the page asking for one.
* [WSGI](https://wsgi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/what.html) is the Python world's standard for how a web server hands a request to an application. This course is JavaScript end to end, so you'll only need it if you land on a Python team.
